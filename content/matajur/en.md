With its cone-shaped outline, Matajur can be recognised from all over the Friulian plain. At 1641 metres it is the emblematic mountain of the Natisone Valleys, and it marks the border between Italy and Slovenia: the summit is on the Italian side, while the northern slope drops towards Caporetto (Kobarid) and the Soča valley.

## The name

The name comes from the Latin *Mons maior*, "the higher mountain", which over the centuries became Mont Major, Matajor and finally Matajur; it only appears in that form on maps from the 18th century. In the local Slovene dialect it is also called Velika Baba, "the great rocky peak". Other names are heard in the valleys too, such as Monte Re in Italian and Mataiûr in Friulian.

## A border summit

According to tradition, the Lombard king Alboin climbed it to gaze at the plain he was about to conquer, although some scholars think of Mount Nanos in Slovenia instead. What is certain is that the summit has been borderland for centuries: with Austria, then Yugoslavia, and today Slovenia.

During the First World War, Matajur was part of the last Italian line of defence. In the Battle of Caporetto, the young lieutenant Erwin Rommel launched his attack on 24 October 1917, crossed the Kolovrat ridge and the Savogna valley and, after some 52 hours, took the summit held by the Salerno Brigade, capturing nearly 9000 prisoners. It was one of the decisive episodes of the Italian retreat.

## Climbing to the summit

The ascent is easy: the slopes are covered with woods and meadows right to the top, and the gradient stays moderate. The most convenient starting point is the **Pelizzo hut** (1320 m), reached by car on the paved road up from Savogna; from there the **Sentiero naturalistico del Matajur** forms a loop of about 6 km that passes over the summit. From Mersino, mule tracks and paths through meadows and woods reach the top in about an hour and a half.

To start from the valley floor, there are several CAI trails:

- **725**, from the village of Stupizza on the Natisone (about 200 m), up the south-west ridge past the Mersino pastures;
- **736**, from Cepletischis, which reaches the summit from the east and then descends to the Pelizzo hut;
- **749**, from San Pietro al Natisone, which passes near the Dom na Matajure hut;
- the **Via Palma (724)**, from the Stupizza pass: very steep, for experienced hikers only.

On the Slovenian side, a trail climbs from the village of Avsa, above Livek.

## At the top

On the summit stands the small church of Christ the Redeemer, rebuilt on the ruins of a chapel inaugurated in 1901 and destroyed during the war. The view stretches from the Karst to Istria and the Grado lagoon, and to the peaks of Kanin, Mangart, Triglav, Krn and the Dolomites; on very clear days you can see Venice. A small astronomical observatory, opened in 2013, stands near the summit.

## Nature

More than 600 plant species grow on the massif: in spring and summer the high meadows fill with narcissi, gentians, asphodels and the Carniolan lily (*Lilium carniolicum*). With a little luck you may see roe deer, red deer, foxes and black grouse, and griffon vultures and eagles sometimes pass through. Its slopes give rise to the springs and streams that feed the Natisone.

## Good to know

By tradition, the Mountain Festival takes place on the summit on the first Sunday of September: check the dates locally. Mountain weather changes quickly: bring water and a warm layer, even in summer.
